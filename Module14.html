Matter, heat & fluids

Grade 9 Physical Science, Module 14. Drag the sliders, drop things into water and watch what the particles do. Every idea comes with a real example.

Everything is made of moving particles

All matter is built from tiny particles that never stop moving. The hotter something is, the faster they move. Slide the energy up and watch ice turn into water, then steam. Notice the temperature graph goes flat while the state changes: the energy is busy breaking the attractions between particles (latent heat).

Solid: fixed shape and volume. Particles vibrate in place.
Liquid: fixed volume, changing shape. Particles slide past each other.
Gas: changing shape and volume. Particles fly freely, far apart.

Temperature is not thermal energy

Temperature is the average kinetic energy of the particles. Thermal energy is the total for all of them, so it also depends on how much stuff there is. Set both to the same temperature and compare.

Real example: a cup of tea and a swimming pool can both be 40 °C, but the pool could heat your whole house for days. That is why a huge iceberg holds more thermal energy than a boiling kettle.

Test yourself

Think first, then open the answer.

Why does a metal spoon feel colder than a wooden spoon at room temperature?

Metal is a better conductor. Heat flows quickly from your hand into the metal, so it feels colder.

Hot tea and a swimming pool are both 40 °C. Which has more thermal energy?

The pool. It has far more particles (greater mass), so more total thermal energy.

Using particles, what happens when ice melts at 0 °C even though the temperature isn’t rising?

The energy breaks the attractions holding the solid structure together. The temperature stays constant until all the ice has melted.

How does convection help heat a room when the heater is near the floor?

Warm air near the heater rises and cooler air sinks to take its place. This convection current circulates the heat.

Density: why things sink or float

Fluids are liquids and gases: anything that flows. Density is how much mass is packed into a volume: D = m ÷ V. Pure water is about 1.0 g/cm³. Denser than the fluid means it sinks, less dense means it floats. Archimedes’ principle says the buoyant force equals the weight of the fluid pushed aside.

Try your own: mass g, volume cm³

Real examples: ice floats because it is less dense than water. In the Dead Sea the salty water is so dense that people float easily. A steel ship floats because its hull is full of air, so its average density is below water’s.

Pressure grows with depth

Pressure is force ÷ area (P = F ÷ A), measured in pascals. In a fluid it rises with depth and with density, and at any depth it pushes equally in every direction. Move the diver.

Real example: at the bottom of a 3 m pool you already feel it in your ears. At about 10 m, the water alone adds one whole atmosphere, which is why scuba divers equalize their ears.

Pascal’s principle: a small push, a big lift

Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is passed on equally everywhere. So a small force on a small piston makes the same pressure on a big piston, and that pressure times a bigger area gives a bigger force.

Real examples: hydraulic car lifts, car brakes, excavator arms and dentist chairs. The catch is you push the small piston much farther than the big one rises, so energy is never created.

Viscosity: how thick is the fluid?

Viscosity is a fluid’s resistance to flowing. Drop three identical balls and see how fluid thickness changes the fall (times are scaled for the animation).

Real examples: honey is thousands of times more viscous than water and cooking oil is roughly fifty times more. Warm honey flows faster, because heating lowers viscosity. That is why engine oil is rated for hot and cold weather.

Test yourself

Think first, then open the answer.

A 50 g object has a volume of 25 cm³. What is its density, and does it float in water?

D = 50 ÷ 25 = 2.0 g/cm³. It sinks because it is denser than water (1.0 g/cm³).

Why do your ears feel more pressure deeper in a pool?

Pressure increases with depth. More water above means greater force per area on your ears.

How does a hydraulic lift raise a car with a small force?

A small force on a small piston creates a pressure that is transmitted equally. On the larger piston, that pressure produces a larger force that can lift the car.

Why does a steel ship float even though steel is denser than water?

The ship’s average density, including the air inside the hull, is less than water’s, so the buoyant force can support its weight.
